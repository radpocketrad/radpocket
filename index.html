Knee Radiography

A practical approach to positioning, image quality and systematic assessment of the AP and lateral knee — with an emergency-radiology focus on subtle fractures, effusion and injuries that are easy to overlook.

Merrill's AtlasBontragerRaby · A&E RadiologyRCRACR

Start with two views

For the standard knee series, use the AP and lateral projections as the foundation of assessment. Additional projections are selected according to the clinical question and local protocol.

  • AP: overall bony alignment, joint spaces and tibial plateau.
  • Lateral: patella, effusion, alignment and subtle fracture signs.
  • Do not let an abnormality on one view stop the search on the other.

Trauma mindset

In acute injury, deliberately search beyond the site of maximum tenderness. A small avulsion, plateau impaction or joint effusion can be the clue to a more important injury.

Useful habit: quality → alignment → bones → joints → soft tissues → subtle signs.

POSITIONING

AP & Lateral Knee

Use local departmental technique where it differs. The following is a learning framework cross-checked against standard positioning references.

AP knee

  • Patient supine or erect according to clinical situation and department protocol.
  • Leg extended as tolerated; position the knee so the detector is centred to the joint.
  • Adjust limb rotation to demonstrate the knee joint and tibial plateau appropriately rather than relying on a fixed rotation for every patient.
  • Include distal femur, proximal tibia/fibula and surrounding soft tissues.
  • Collimate to the area of interest while preserving required anatomy.
Evaluate: joint-space appearance, tibial plateaux, intercondylar region, femoral condyles, proximal fibula and overall alignment.

Lateral knee

  • True lateral positioning is the goal; adapt the position to pain and trauma.
  • For trauma, a horizontal-beam lateral may be required by local protocol.
  • Include distal femur, proximal tibia/fibula, patella and soft tissues.
  • Check femoral-condyle congruity on the lateral image.
  • Adequate exposure is important for detecting an effusion or lipohaemarthrosis.
RCR trauma adequacy: the RCR audit standard assesses exposure and congruity of the medial and lateral femoral condyles on horizontal-beam lateral trauma images.

Additional projections — when clinically indicated

Patellar or intercondylar/tunnel projections may be added according to the suspected injury and local protocol. Do not treat an additional view as a substitute for clinical assessment or the standard views.

Trauma tip: if clinical concern remains despite apparently normal standard views, consider whether further imaging or additional projection is appropriate rather than assuming the injury is excluded.

SYSTEMATIC ASSESSMENT

Read the Knee Like a Trauma Study

A practical sequence derived from emergency-radiology principles and cross-checked against RCR/ACR guidance.

1 · Image qualityCoverage, exposure, side marker, positioning and whether the lateral is sufficiently true/adequate for the clinical question.
2 · AlignmentFemorotibial alignment, patellar position and overall relationship of the distal femur to proximal tibia.
3 · BonesTrace cortices systematically: distal femur → patella → tibial plateau/spines → proximal tibia → fibula.
4 · JointsAssess medial/lateral compartments, patellofemoral relationship and joint-space symmetry/appearance.
5 · Soft tissuesLook for suprapatellar effusion, fat–fluid level, soft-tissue swelling and abnormal fat planes.
6 · Subtle signsSearch deliberately for plateau depression, small avulsion fragments, cortical step, impacted bone and occult clues.

🚨 Don't miss

  • Tibial plateau fracture: subtle surface irregularity, depression, impaction/sclerosis or abnormal tibial margin.
  • Fat–fluid level: on an appropriate horizontal-beam lateral, can indicate intra-articular fracture.
  • Patellar fracture: inspect the patella carefully on both available projections; an additional projection may be needed when clinically indicated.
  • Segond-type avulsion: small lateral tibial avulsion fragment can be an important associated finding.
  • Fibular head/neck injury: deliberately inspect it rather than stopping at the tibial plateau.

Four features for a tibial plateau search

  1. Is the plateau smooth and continuous?
  2. Is there focal increased subchondral density suggesting impaction?
  3. Is there abnormal lateral tibial margin displacement?
  4. On the lateral, is there an effusion or fat–fluid level?

These are teaching features from Raby's emergency-radiology approach. They are not a substitute for formal reporting or local protocols.

Raby principle: finding one abnormality should not end the search. Continue through the entire image and then cross-check the second projection.

Trauma imaging pathway

For acute trauma, the clinical decision to image should be considered alongside decision rules and examination findings. ACR guidance supports knee radiography in several acute-trauma scenarios, including when focal tenderness, effusion or inability to bear weight is present. When radiographs are negative but occult fracture or internal derangement remains suspected, MRI without contrast is a commonly appropriate next study; CT can be appropriate in selected fracture scenarios.

Clinical context matters: radiographic appearance alone does not decide whether further imaging is needed.
TEACH

How I Read a Trauma Knee

Use the same sequence every time until it becomes automatic.

1
Check the image
Side marker, coverage, exposure, rotation and whether the projections answer the clinical question.
2
Check alignment
Femorotibial relationship, patellar position and obvious dislocation/subluxation.
3
Trace the bones
Follow every visible cortex. Look for small steps, lucencies, depression, avulsion fragments and impaction.
4
Inspect the joints
Medial and lateral compartments, patellofemoral relationship and the tibial plateau.
5
Look at soft tissues
Effusion, suprapatellar fullness, fat–fluid level and swelling can provide the clue when the fracture itself is subtle.
6
Cross-check the other view
An AP abnormality should be tested on the lateral and vice versa. Never stop after the first finding.
7
Decide what happens next
Use the clinical picture, local pathway and relevant guidance to decide whether additional views or cross-sectional imaging are warranted.

Pittsburgh Knee Rule — clinical checkpoint

The RCR describes the Pittsburgh Knee Rules as a tool for triaging traumatic knee pain. Its listed criteria include traumatic knee pain with age under 12 or over 50, or inability to walk four weight-bearing steps in the ED. Use the current local/clinical pathway when applying decision rules.

Horizontal-beam lateral: why it matters

In trauma, the horizontal-beam lateral can help demonstrate an effusion and lipohaemarthrosis. RCR adequacy guidance emphasises adequate exposure and femoral-condyle congruity for this purpose.
