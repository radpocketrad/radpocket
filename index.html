THE ANKLE SERIES

Think in three views

AP, Mortise and Lateral are complementary projections. Reviewing them together gives a more complete assessment of ankle alignment, bones and joint spaces.

Key message: Each projection answers a different question. Assess image quality first, then compare all three views before focusing on a specific abnormality.
APFrontal view

Demonstrates the distal tibia and fibula, malleoli, talus and overall frontal ankle alignment.

Key focusOverall bony alignment and ankle joint space.
MortiseOblique view

Demonstrates the ankle mortise with reduced malleolar superimposition and allows focused assessment of the joint spaces.

Key focusMortise configuration and distal tibiofibular relationship.
LateralSagittal view

Provides complementary assessment of the distal tibia and fibula, talus, calcaneus and hindfoot relationships.

Key focusTalar alignment, hindfoot and posterior structures.
Three views. A more complete picture. Review AP, Mortise and Lateral together and correlate imaging findings with the clinical information.

🚑 Trauma considerations

  • Avoid unnecessary movement of the injured limb.
  • Do not force dorsiflexion or rotation when this causes pain or may compromise the injury.
  • Adapt positioning to the patient's clinical condition and tolerance.
  • Use trauma or horizontal-beam techniques when clinically indicated and in accordance with local protocols.
  • Communicate clearly with the patient and clinical team before moving or repositioning the limb.

AP Ankle Positioning

Central Ray

① Patient position

  • Patient supine on the X-ray table.
  • Leg extended and supported.
  • Foot positioned for an AP ankle projection.
  • Avoid unnecessary rotation.

② Central ray

  • CR perpendicular to the detector.
  • Centre to the ankle joint / midpoint between the malleoli.
  • Collimate to the area of interest.

③ AP Ankle Evaluation

  • Lower third of the tibia and fibula included.
  • Medial and lateral malleoli demonstrated.
  • Talar dome and tibiotalar joint space visible.
  • True AP appearance with no unwanted internal or external rotation.
  • Appropriate centring, collimation, exposure and bony detail.

⚠️ Common positioning errors

  • Unwanted internal or external rotation.
  • Failure to include the distal tibia and fibula.
  • Suboptimal foot positioning where dorsiflexion is achievable.
Correction:

Recheck leg and foot alignment, centring and collimation before exposure.

💡 Positioning pearl: Compare the malleoli and overall ankle alignment to help identify unwanted rotation.

Mortise Positioning

Central Ray

① Patient position

  • Patient supine with the leg extended.
  • Dorsiflex the foot where possible.
  • Internally rotate the entire leg and foot 15°–20°.
  • Aim to place the intermalleolar line parallel to the detector.

② Central ray

  • CR perpendicular to the detector.
  • Centre to the ankle joint, midway between the malleoli.
  • Collimate to include the distal tibia, fibula and talus.

③ Mortise Evaluation

  • Ankle mortise joint space demonstrated.
  • Medial, lateral and superior joint spaces assessed.
  • Distal tibia and fibula included.
  • Minimal tibiofibular overlap at the distal tibiofibular joint.
  • Assess positioning, rotation and overall image quality.

⚠️ Common positioning errors

  • Insufficient internal rotation, leaving the mortise inadequately demonstrated.
  • Excessive rotation causing inappropriate overlap.
  • Rotating the foot without rotating the whole lower limb consistently.
Correction:

Rotate the leg and foot together and reassess the intermalleolar alignment.

💡 Positioning pearl: Rotate the whole leg and foot together to avoid unwanted rotation at the knee or ankle.

Lateral Ankle Positioning

Central Ray

① Patient position

  • Patient positioned lateral recumbent or seated as tolerated.
  • Affected side placed against the detector.
  • Adjust the ankle to a true lateral position.
  • Dorsiflex the foot where possible and support the opposite limb clear of the field.

② Central ray

  • CR perpendicular to the detector.
  • Centre to the ankle joint.
  • Centre the central ray at the medial malleolus, perpendicular to the detector.
  • Collimate to include the distal tibia and fibula, talus and calcaneus.

③ Lateral Ankle Evaluation

  • Lower third of the tibia and fibula included.
  • Talus and tibiotalar joint demonstrated in lateral projection.
  • Talar domes/trochlear surfaces appropriately superimposed for a true lateral.
  • Distal fibula projects over the posterior half of the tibia.
  • Calcaneus, navicular and base of the 5th metatarsal included.
  • Posterior malleolar region and relevant hindfoot anatomy demonstrated.
  • Assess positioning, rotation, exposure and overall image quality.

⚠️ Common positioning errors

  • Not achieving a true lateral position.
  • Excessive rotation of the lower leg.
  • Inadequate demonstration of the required ankle and hindfoot anatomy.
Correction:

Support the patient comfortably and adjust positioning to minimise rotation without unnecessary movement.

💡 Positioning pearl: Aim for a true lateral by minimising rotation and achieving appropriate superimposition of the ankle structures.

ANKLE · ASSESSMENT

Assess the ankle systematically

Use a structured approach to check image quality, alignment, bones, joints and soft tissues before moving on to specific pathology.

Image quality & positioning

  • Correct side marker present.
  • Entire ankle joint included.
  • Distal tibia and fibula included.
  • Talus and relevant hindfoot anatomy demonstrated.
  • Adequate exposure and no significant motion artefact.
Projection-specific checks

AP: assess the distal tibiofibular relationship and tibiotalar joint.

Mortise: assess the mortise, joint-space symmetry and distal tibiofibular relationship.

Lateral: assess the lateral relationship of the distal fibula and tibia and demonstrate the tibiotalar joint.

Alignment

  • Talar dome centred beneath the tibial plafond.
  • Medial, lateral and superior joint spaces assessed.
  • Distal tibiofibular relationship assessed.
  • Look for abnormal talar tilt or displacement.

Bones

Trace the cortex systematically.

  • Distal tibia and medial malleolus.
  • Fibula and lateral malleolus.
  • Talus and talar dome.
  • Calcaneus and visible hindfoot anatomy.
  • Look for cortical interruption, fracture lines, displacement or angulation.

Joints

  • Tibiotalar joint.
  • Distal tibiofibular joint.
  • Subtalar joint, particularly on the lateral view.
  • Assess joint-space width and symmetry.
  • Look at the talar dome for focal irregularity or an osteochondral abnormality.

Soft tissues

  • Look for soft-tissue swelling.
  • Assess for joint effusion where visible.
  • Look for soft-tissue abnormality or foreign body where relevant.

Trauma & common abnormalities

Fracture

Look for cortical disruption, a fracture line, displacement, angulation and small avulsion fragments.

Mortise disruption

Look for widened medial clear space, abnormal talar position and widening of the distal tibiofibular relationship.

Degenerative change

Look for joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and cystic change.

🚑 Ottawa Ankle Rules

For acute ankle trauma, the Ottawa Ankle Rules are a clinical decision rule used to help determine whether ankle radiographs are indicated in appropriate patients.

  • Bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the lateral malleolus.
  • Bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the medial malleolus.
  • Inability to bear weight both immediately after the injury and in the clinical setting, or inability to walk four steps.

Important: This is a clinical decision rule, not a substitute for clinical assessment or local imaging/referral protocols. The ACR notes exclusion criteria and validates the rule for adults and children over 5 years in appropriate acute-trauma settings.

🔎 Trauma — don't miss

  • Inspect the talar dome for osteochondral injury.
  • Check the syndesmosis and distal tibiofibular relationship.
  • Look carefully for posterior malleolar, talar neck or talar process injuries.
  • Check the calcaneus, navicular and base of the 5th metatarsal where visible.
  • If there is evidence of mortise instability, consider the possibility of a more proximal fibular injury such as a Maisonneuve pattern and correlate clinically.
  • Always review AP, Mortise and Lateral together.

LESSON 1

Why do we take three ankle projections?

Each projection provides complementary information. Avoid treating the AP, Mortise and Lateral as three isolated pictures.

AP

Provides an overall frontal assessment of the distal tibia, distal fibula, malleoli and tibiotalar relationship.

Think: overall alignment

Mortise

Helps demonstrate the ankle mortise and allows assessment of joint-space symmetry and the distal tibiofibular relationship.

Think: mortise alignment

Lateral

Provides a lateral assessment of the distal tibia, talus and hindfoot, including the tibiotalar and subtalar regions.

Think: lateral relationships

How to read an ankle X-ray

LESSON 3

Trauma reasoning — don't just look where it hurts

In trauma, a systematic search is especially important. Trace the cortex and assess alignment rather than stopping when you find the first abnormality.

🦴 Trace the cortex

Follow the visible cortical margins of the distal tibia, fibula, malleoli and talus. Look for interruption, fracture lines and small fragments.

↔️ Check alignment

Assess talar position within the mortise and look for abnormal displacement, tilt or widening of relevant relationships.

🔎 Look beyond the obvious

Small avulsion fragments, subtle cortical breaks and abnormalities away from the reported pain can be easy to miss.

🩻 Compare all views

Use AP, Mortise and Lateral together. A finding that is subtle on one projection may be clearer on another.

Clinical thinking checkpoint

A patient presents following an ankle injury. You notice a small possible cortical irregularity on one view. What should you do next?

Think systematically. Review the finding on the other projections, trace the surrounding cortex, reassess alignment and inspect the remainder of the ankle series. Do not stop the search after finding one possible abnormality.
LESSON 4

Build the habit

Before calling an ankle series normal, mentally complete the sequence:

Image quality → Alignment → Bones → Cartilage/joints → Soft tissues → Compare all projections.
